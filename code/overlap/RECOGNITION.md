# Polynomial recognition at the exact affine maximum

A *point* is a variable and a *row* is a triple. Assume four perfect matchings of linear triples on a nonempty finite point set. A row neighborhood of a point is its four incident rows. The target is exact equality to 5V/12, with no rounding.

## Tiles

The extremal component inequality proved in next_flat is strict for every nontrivial connected bipartite graph except K4,4: for s<8 the floor(s²/4) bound is strict against 16(s−1)/7; for s>8 use 2s; at s=8 equality requires 16 edges and hence K4,4. A tile is a K4,4 with 16 cross pairs arising from 16 distinct rows. Its footprint is this 16-element row set. Every one of its eight points uses all its four incident rows, exactly one cross pair in each. Choosing one pair per row at equality is precisely covering the rows exactly once by such footprints. For the reverse implication, disjoint footprints force disjoint selected point sets since each selected point uses its entire row neighborhood; choose any one witnessing tile per chosen footprint. The selected components are K4,4 and isolated points. Their opposite-pair equations specify the extremal flat. Coordinates of isolated points are independent, as is one phase per K4,4.

All footprints can be found in polynomial time, and their number is linear. At a point p on one shore choose the opposite point in each of its four rows (16 choices). The four resulting points have at most eight common neighbors. Enumerate three further common neighbors to accompany p, testing that the 16 pairs belong to 16 distinct rows. Enumerate p and discard duplicate footprints, keeping one witness.

## Rigidity relative to any solution

Fix any exact solution. Call its selected tiles old, its selected points active, and its remaining points free. Every row (a,b,z) has its two active members a,b on opposite shores of the same old tile F and its unique free member z. An active point needs all four incident rows for its four selected edges, so a third member of a row cannot itself be active. No two free points are adjacent in the point shadow. The old active points have precisely the old K4,4 components in their induced shadow. At each active v, let U(v) be its four distinct free neighbors.

**Lemma.** Every admissible tile either is old or has one shore U consisting entirely of free points and its other shore consisting entirely of active points v with U(v)=U. The footprint of the latter tile is uniquely determined by U: it consists of the 16 rows through U. Different free quadruples give different footprints, since each row has a unique free point.

Indeed, if all eight vertices are active, connectivity and the old active shadow force the old tile. Any free vertices in an alternative must lie on the same shore because free points are mutually nonadjacent. Suppose this shore also has an active point a. Its four opposite active neighbors must comprise the opposite shore of its old tile. Any free point z in the mixed shore uses all four original rows to meet those four distinct neighbors. Each such row has a second active point on a's old shore. These four second active points are distinct by linearity of the pair (z,second), so one is a. In that row the new tile has both pairs a–b and z–b, contradicting 16 distinct rows. The stated description and footprint formula follow.

Thus each row (a,b,z) occurs in at most three distinct footprints: old F, the potential footprint determined by U(a), and the potential footprint determined by U(b). Necessity holds regardless of which other tiles one might select. In particular, rejecting any raw row frequency above three is sound, provided duplicate footprints have been merged.

## Elimination and the connected dichotomy

Let C(r) be the set of still eligible footprints at row r. Reject an empty C(r). Whenever two rows have nonempty option sets C(s) strictly contained in C(r), delete every footprint in C(r) minus C(s) globally. This preserves all solutions because exactly one option in C(s) must be chosen. Iterate to a fixed point. Every old footprint of the chosen solution survives, although some alternative footprints may disappear.

In a YES component, whenever r=(a,b,z) still has three options they must be F, U(a) and U(b), denoting the latter two footprints by their free quadruples. All are distinct. Each of the four rows incident to a contains F and U(a). Each row incident to b contains F and U(b). Each row incident to z contains U(a) and U(b), since both full footprints contain z's whole neighborhood. After elimination a row adjacent to r can therefore have neither one option nor two: two would be a proper subset of C(r). Adjacency through points connects the row component. Thus at a fixed point a connected YES component has either all frequencies at most two or all frequencies exactly three.

## A homogeneous component has at most 64 rows

Assume now that a YES component has exactly three options at every row after elimination. Every active v in its old block F lies in two incident footprints F and U(v) that contain every row through v. A free point z similarly has a fixed pair U,W of distinct alternative footprints: each group with label U containing z requires four distinct active neighbors of z with free-neighbor set exactly U. The eight old active neighbors of z are all distinct by linearity. Since at any row through z two distinct groups exist, these eight neighbors divide four and four. Thus the pair at z is independent of the chosen row, and each alternative U has precisely four possible active points on its opposite shore: fix any z in U to see uniqueness. Assign each point the indicated pair of labels. In a row with labels F,U,W its three points carry each of the three unordered pairs exactly once.

In this regime an alternative footprint cannot coincide with an old footprint: a row of the alternative tile has an original active endpoint belonging to that alternative, which collapses two of its three possible labels if the old footprint is the same. For any still eligible footprint H its rows can be arranged in a 4×4 grid, with the two shores of its tile as row and column indices. For old H its eight spoke points are its eight old active vertices. For alternative H its eight spoke points are its four free points and the four unique active points of that alternative. In either case each spoke p has attached pair {H,q(p)}. The cell with indices i,j consequently has exactly the three distinct labels H,q(i),q(j). Repetitions among q-values on one side are allowed. A move across a spoke preserves that index and can change the other to any of the four possibilities.

Consider states consisting of a row plus an ordering of its three distinct labels. In a move of type i, travel across the unique original point whose pair fixes the other two ordered labels. Carry those two labels in the same positions and fill position i with the remaining label at the new row. A state has at most four choices of destination for each type, including staying still. Any consecutive moves of distinct types preserve the third labeled footprint H and have intermediate and final rows in its same 4×4 grid. The first move fixes one spoke and the second must fix the perpendicular spoke as the fixed label besides H is now the label in the other position. Reading the other two sides of this rectangle instead produces the same terminal row with the same three ordered labels, with move types interchanged. Consecutive moves of a single type fix the same point at the intermediate state by uniqueness of each pair there, and collapse to one move.

Bubble-sort any finite word in the move types and then collapse consecutive equal types. Every reachable ordered state is therefore reachable by at most one move of each type in order 1,2,3. Allowing stays, there are at most 4³ = 64 possibilities. Starting from one ordered row, every row in the connected component is the projection of a reachable state because any adjacency across a point is an allowed move. The component has at most 64 rows. In fact there are at most 12 eligible footprints since each has 16 rows and each row three labels.

## Recognition

Handle row-connected components independently. Reject an empty row option or any initial row with more than three distinct options. Perform subset elimination. A mixed region with both a three-option and a lower-frequency row is impossible in a connected YES component. If all frequencies are at most two, a row with one option forces that footprint true; a row with two options imposes that their two Boolean selection bits have XOR one. Solve these equations, e.g. by parity propagation with pins. If all rows have three options, reject any component larger than 64 rows. In every other homogeneous three-option component enumerate at most 2^12 choices of footprints. Accept exactly on a partition; recover the affine flat from selected tile witnesses. On NO there is no risk of a false acceptance because the returned exact partition is directly verifiable. Straightforward pairwise subset scans are polynomial; adjacency-only scans suffice for the connected dichotomy. Thus the problem of attaining the exact bound has a deterministic polynomial-time search and decision algorithm. The known NP-completeness for a sublinear additive tolerance concerns a different threshold.

**Counting corollary.** The number of distinct flats attaining 5V/12 is also computable in deterministic polynomial time. Each row of an extremal flat has a unique permanently opposite pair: its third point is an independent free coordinate. Indeed, the envelope of the chosen pair equations has the same dimension as the extremal flat contained in it, so the two are equal. Thus each extremal flat determines precisely one selected K4,4 graph and conversely. Give each distinct footprint a positive integer weight equal to the number of its distinct witnessing tiles (the two shores are unordered). Each feasible footprint selection contributes the product of the selected weights, since disjoint footprints allow independent representations. For two-option components a connected parity constraint graph has at most two choices, including singleton pins. Sum the associated products and multiply over parity components. For homogeneous three-option components use the same weighted sum over at most 2^12 choices. Different row components multiply. The total is bounded by three choices of selected pair per row, so it has polynomial bit length. A witness from the same procedure also gives an explicit extremal flat. The proof concerns exact equality and applies with zero count whenever 5V/12 is not integral.

**Sharp finite size.** Take points A(i,j),B(j,k),C(k,i) for i,j,k from the binary field of order four. Take the 64 rows (A(i,j),B(j,k),C(k,i)), grouped by s=i+j+k. Every group is a perfect matching and the rows are linear. Original tiles use A/B at fixed j. For an alternative, the free-neighbor sets of A(i,j) and B(j,k) are exactly the planes fixing i and k. The 12 footprints are the coordinate planes, and exactly three tilings partition the graph by fixing one of the three coordinates. Every row has three options and all 64 belong to its connected component. The bound cannot be decreased.
